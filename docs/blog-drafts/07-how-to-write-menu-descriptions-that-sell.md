Your menu is the most-read piece of writing your restaurant will ever produce. Every single guest reads it, usually while they are hungry and ready to spend money. And yet most menus are written as if the only goal is to list what exists. "Chicken sandwich. Caesar salad. Fries."

A menu can do so much more than catalog your food. Written well, it makes dishes sound irresistible, nudges diners toward the plates you most want to sell, and quietly raises your average check. Here is how to write descriptions that actually do that.

## Descriptions sell, names just label

Compare two ways of putting the same dish on a menu.

"Grilled chicken sandwich."

Versus: "Charred buttermilk-marinated chicken, smoked aioli, pickled red onion, brioche bun."

Same sandwich. The second one makes your mouth water. The difference is not fancier food. It is words that let the diner taste the dish before it arrives. Sensory, specific language does the selling.

You do not need to do this for every item. You need to do it for the ones that earn it: your signatures, your highest-margin plates, and the dishes you most want to be known for.

## Use words that make people taste it

The descriptions that work lean on a few simple levers.

**Sensory detail.** Words that evoke taste, texture, and aroma. Crispy, smoky, velvety, charred, slow-roasted, tangy. These let the reader experience the dish in their head.

**Specificity.** "House-made" beats "fresh." "24-hour braised short rib" beats "tender beef." Specific details signal care and quality, and they are more believable than vague praise.

**Provenance and method, when it is true.** Where something comes from or how it is made adds value, but only if it is real. "Local" and "house-made" are powerful right up until a diner discovers they are not true. Never write a description you cannot stand behind.

The line to walk is between appetizing and overwrought. A description that reads like a poem is as off-putting as one that reads like a spreadsheet. A few well-chosen words usually beat a paragraph.

## Guide the eye toward what you want to sell

A menu is not just words. It is a layout, and layout influences choice. Diners do not read a menu top to bottom like a book. Their eyes jump around, landing on whatever you have made visually prominent.

That gives you a quiet tool. The dishes you box, highlight, place at the top of a section, or pair with a photo get noticed and ordered more often. Use that placement for your most profitable and most memorable items, not just whatever happened to be listed first.

Be careful with how you present prices too. Long columns of dollar signs lined up on the right train diners to scan for the cheapest option. Tucking the price at the end of the description, in the same size text, keeps the focus on the food instead of the cost. ## Keep it consistent with your brand

Your menu is a brand document, not just a list. The voice of your descriptions should match the rest of your restaurant. A playful neighborhood taco spot and a refined tasting-menu restaurant should not sound the same on the page. The words, the length, the formality all signal what kind of experience a guest is about to have.

This is why menu writing is really part of branding. A menu that matches your identity, your design, and your voice feels intentional, and intentional feels worth paying for. It is one piece of a larger [restaurant branding](/services/restaurant-branding) system that includes your logo, your colors, your photography, and the overall feel a guest gets the moment they look at your menu.

## A quick checklist

When you next revisit your menu, run each item past a few questions:

- Does this description make the dish sound appetizing, or just name it?
- Have I used specific, sensory words for my signature and high-margin items?
- Is everything I have written actually true?
- Are my best plates visually prominent, not buried?
- Does the whole thing sound like my restaurant?

Small rewrites here often pay off faster than almost any other change, because every guest reads the result.
