Every time a customer places an order through Uber Eats, Grubhub, or DoorDash, your restaurant hands over anywhere from 15 to 30 percent of that sale in commission fees. On a $40 order, that is up to $12 gone before you have paid for food, labor, or rent.

For most independent restaurants, that is not sustainable. And yet the apps have become so embedded in how people order that walking away entirely feels impossible.

The solution is not to abandon third-party platforms overnight. It is to systematically shift your customers, and your revenue, toward channels you actually control. Here is exactly how to do it.

First, understand what you are really paying

Before you can fix the problem, you need to see it clearly. Most owners know they are paying commissions. Few have calculated the actual annual cost.

Here is a quick exercise. Pull your monthly statements from each delivery platform. Add up the total commission fees across Uber Eats, Grubhub, and DoorDash. Multiply by 12. That number is what you pay for the convenience of being listed on someone else's app.

For a restaurant doing $15,000 a month in third-party delivery at an average 25 percent commission, that is $45,000 a year in fees. That is not a line item. That is a salary. That is a kitchen renovation. That is your profit margin.

Strategy 1: Launch your own direct ordering system

This is the foundation of everything else on this list. If customers cannot order directly from you online, every other tactic is just damage control.

A direct ordering system lets customers place pickup and delivery orders through your own website, and you keep the revenue minus a small payment processing fee (typically 2 to 3 percent) instead of a 15 to 30 percent commission.

For the delivery side, our own Turnerr Deliver service dispatches the nearest driver the moment an order comes in and charges a flat fee per delivered order rather than a percentage, so a busy delivery night no longer means a giant commission bill.

The platform matters less than the decision to actually launch one. Every day without a direct ordering channel is another day of paying full commission on orders you could have kept. We handle this setup end to end as part of our commission-free deliveries service.

Strategy 2: Win the Google search battle

Here is the most overlooked opportunity in restaurant marketing. When a customer searches "Chinese food delivery near me" or "best burger restaurant in [your city]," they usually end up on a third-party app. Not because they prefer it, but because Uber Eats and DoorDash have spent millions ranking for those searches.

You can beat them at a local level. Three moves:

Optimize your Google Business Profile. This is free and powerful. Make sure your profile has current hours, a complete menu, quality photos, and a direct link to your online ordering page. When someone finds you on Google Maps, they should land on your site, not an app. This is the heart of our Google Business Profile optimization service.

Run targeted Google Ads. For a few dollars a day, you can run search ads that appear when people in your delivery zone search for food. The cost per click is almost always less than the commission you would pay on the resulting order. A $3 click that produces a $45 direct order beats a "free" Uber Eats order that costs you $11 in commission. This is exactly what our Google Ads service is built to do.

Invest in local SEO. Build location pages, earn reviews, and publish content targeting "[food type] delivery in [your area]" searches. This is a longer game, but the payoff is organic traffic flowing straight to your ordering page month after month with no ad spend. That is the core of our restaurant SEO work.

Strategy 3: Convert app customers into direct customers

Every order that comes through a third-party app is a missed opportunity, unless you use it as a conversion tool. The customer already likes your food. You just need to show them a better way to order it.

Tactics that work:

  • Put a card in every delivery bag. "Order direct at yoursite.com and save 10 percent on your next order." Simple, cheap, and surprisingly effective.
  • Offer a first-time direct discount. A one-time code for customers who switch to ordering from your site.
  • Create direct-only menu items. A secret menu, a combo, or a dessert available only when people order from your own website.

The apps do not own your customers. They rent you access to them. Your job is to build a direct relationship before the next order.

Strategy 4: Build an email and SMS list

This is your long-term insurance against platform dependency. Every customer email or phone number is a free, direct line that no algorithm or commission structure can touch.

Collect contact information at every touchpoint: through your direct ordering system (most capture it automatically), at the register with a quick "leave your email for exclusive deals" prompt, through a landing page linked from social, and through a WiFi login page in the restaurant.

Then actually use the list. A weekly text with the special of the day and a direct ordering link. A monthly email with new menu items. A flash promotion: "Order in the next two hours and get free delivery." Every message that drives a direct order is commission money saved.

Strategy 5: Use social media to drive direct orders

Your Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok accounts should serve one primary business goal: getting followers to order directly from you.

That means every bio link points to your ordering page, not your Uber Eats listing. Every post about a new dish, a behind-the-scenes kitchen moment, or a customer review ends with a nudge toward direct ordering. Run the occasional follower-only promotion: "DM us the word DIRECT for a free appetizer code on your next website order." It builds the habit and rewards the behavior you want.

Strategy 6: Negotiate lower commission rates

If you are not ready to cut the apps entirely, at least stop paying full price. The published commission rates are not final. They are starting points.

Restaurants with higher order volume have the most leverage, but even smaller operations can push for better terms. What to ask for:

  • Lower commission tiers. Uber Eats and Grubhub both offer reduced rates (sometimes 15 percent or less) for restaurants willing to handle their own delivery or accept reduced in-app visibility.
  • Promotional credits. Ask for free marketing placements or credits toward promoted listings as part of your agreement.
  • No exclusivity. Do not sign exclusive deals. Being on multiple platforms gives you bargaining power with each one.

Even a 5 percent reduction on $10,000 a month in app orders saves you $6,000 a year.

Strategy 7: Offer your own delivery, or use flat-fee delivery

One reason restaurants stay on third-party platforms is logistics. They do not have their own drivers. But you do not need a fleet of employees to offer delivery.

Flat-fee delivery services let you use third-party drivers without the commission model:

  • DoorDash Drive. Use DoorDash's driver network for a flat per-delivery fee (typically $5 to $9) instead of a percentage.
  • Uber Direct. The same idea through Uber's network.
  • Local couriers. Many cities have independent delivery companies that charge flat rates.

On a $50 order, paying a $7 flat delivery fee is dramatically better than paying $12.50 in commission at 25 percent. You keep the customer relationship, the data, and the bulk of the revenue.

The transition roadmap

You do not have to do all of this at once. A realistic sequence:

Month 1. Launch a direct ordering system on your website. Add your ordering link to your Google Business Profile.

Month 2. Start putting cards in every third-party delivery bag. Set up a basic email and SMS collection system. Update every social bio with your direct ordering link.

Month 3. Launch a Google Ads campaign targeting high-intent delivery searches in your area. Send your first email or SMS campaign with a direct-order promotion.

Month 4 and beyond. Track your direct-to-app order ratio every month. Aim to shift 10 percent more orders to direct channels each quarter. Negotiate lower rates with any platform you keep.
