The 7 terms in this report

Read these once and the rest of the report reads like plain English.

🌱
Organic traffic

Free visitors who find a site through Google (not paid ads). More of it = Google trusts them = free leads every month.

🔑
Ranking keywords ("queries")

How many different Google searches a site shows up for. 50,000 keywords = it appears for 50,000 different searches = a wide net.

⛰️
KD — Keyword Difficulty

How hard a search is to win, 0–100. Under ~30 = realistic for us. 60+ = a fight against giants. We chase the low ones.

📄
SERP

The Google results page for a search. "We rank in the SERP" just means we show up on that page.

🏙️
Programmatic / city pages

One page template multiplied across cities — "Restaurant Web Design in Houston / Dallas / Austin…". One build, hundreds of local-catching pages.

🧲
Lead magnet

A free thing (tool, audit, checklist) that gets an owner to hand over their email. Then we sell to them.

🔗
Backlinks & Authority (DR)

Other sites linking to you — Google treats each like a vote of trust. The 0–100 "DR/Authority" score is built from them. The big agencies' real moat.

Thrive Agency

thriveagency.com · Arlington, TX · ~160 staff
Most omnipresent rival

Shows up in almost every search we care about — both their own pages and inside "best agency" lists. ~200K–300K visits/mo, ~1M backlinks. But organic traffic dropped ~15% in May 2026 — likely Google's AI-search shift hitting their generic content.

What they do better
  • City × industry × service pages at scale ("Dallas Restaurant SEO Company"). Our exact engine, run wider — the #1 tactic to copy.
  • "Best [X] companies" list articles — and they rank themselves #1.
  • Risk-reversal everywhere: "month-to-month, no contracts, 95% retention." Kills the owner's fear of being locked in — sales should be saying this.
  • Trust badges stacked: Inc. 5000 ×7, Google Premier Partner, 4.7★ Clutch.

  • Only ~3 cities have their full restaurant set — we can blanket Texas first.
  • No pricing, generic voice, big-agency feel — we offer owner-level attention.

WebFX

webfx.com · 30+ yrs · 750+ staff · DR 89
Study the playbook

One of the best-ranking agency domains in the US. Hundreds of thousands of free visits for 18K+ keywords. Their trust score (DR 89, 33K linking sites) is 30 years of compounding — we can't match it. So we copy the mechanism, not the size.

What they do better (copyable)
  • Free tools as lead magnets — 28+ of them. Their top pages are tools, not service pages (one calculator pulls ~14K visits/mo). Confirms our Review Calculator strategy — add more.
  • "How much does X cost" pages — high buying intent, low competition. We have zero.
  • "Learn" hubs + glossaries owning "what is…" searches.
  • Original data ("we analyzed 2.3B sessions") earns links cheaply.
  • Partial pricing transparency ("starting at $X/mo").
Not realistically copyable
  • 30 years of backlinks (DR 89, 33K linking domains).
  • 750 staff running 28 tools across 20+ industries.
  • Custom in-house software platforms.

The lesson: don't out-size WebFX. Copy the mechanism — free tool + tight topic cluster + cost pages — aimed at one niche.

📐 Teardown: Vorgestern — our exact strategy, done huge, and failing

The single most useful competitor lesson in this report. They run the same play we do (city + service pages) across all 50 states — and it earns almost nothing. Proof that breadth without depth doesn't rank.

📦 What they built (impressive on paper)

A clean URL machine: /state/city/service/industry — e.g. /texas/houston/web-design/restaurants. An estimated 15,000–40,000+ pages across all 50 states, with tidy breadcrumbs, hub pages, internal linking, and a lead form on every single one.

💀 Why it's failing (~1.4K visits, ~9 keywords total)

The copy is templated — every city page is the same sentences with just the city name and a sector list swapped in. No real local detail, no case studies, no proof, and a Georgia phone number on Texas pages. Google's thin-content systems are quietly burying the whole thing.

The lesson — steal the architecture, not the content: adopt their city × service × industry URL structure and hub linking, but fill every page with real local data and real client proof (we have actual restaurant reports — they have none). Our 10 Texas metros done deep will beat their 50 states done thin. Concentration beats sprawl.

1 · Free tools / lead magnetsiA lead magnet = a free tool/audit/checklist that captures a business owner's email so we can follow up and sell.

The highest-leverage gap on the board — and the most misunderstood. Here's what a free tool is actually for, and why the best-ranking agency in this entire report is built on exactly this.

⚡ Read this first — settle the "traffic vs clients" question
"We don't need empty traffic — we need clients and leads."

That concern is 100% correct. And it's exactly why we should build tools — the right way.

Agreed: traffic for its own sake is worthless. We should never build a tool that pulls visitors who'll never hire us. So the test for any tool is NOT "how much traffic" — it's two things only: does it capture a lead, and does it earn links?

A relevant tool produces clients two ways. (1) It captures the email of a buyer with the exact problem we fix — e.g. an owner who just learned their restaurant isn't showing on Google Maps. That's a lead, not a pageview. (2) It earns the backlinks that make our service & city pages rank at all — and those pages are what bring clients. A new site cannot rank its money-pages without that authority. So tools aren't instead of leads — they're what makes lead-generation possible.

One honest correction for how we pitch it: Google does not rank you higher just for having traffic. The value isn't the traffic itself — it's the leads captured, links earned, and retargeting audience built. A tool with no email capture really would be empty traffic — so we simply won't build that kind.

The proposal, in client terms: build ONE tool — a free "Are you showing on Google Maps?" / Google Business Profile audit — and judge it purely on leads: emails captured + calls booked in 90 days. If it doesn't produce leads, we kill it. Zero risk, and it quietly builds the authority our client-pages need.
📍 Real-world proof

WebFX — the strongest agency domain in our research — runs 28+ free tools. Their single highest-traffic pages are tools, not service pages. One simple free calculator pulls ~14,000 visitors a month on its own. Their whole #1 ranking position is built on this play.

HubSpot's "Website Grader" became one of the most famous lead magnets ever made — millions of submissions, and a cornerstone of how HubSpot grew from startup to a multi-billion-dollar company.

✅ Why it works (3 reasons)

1. It reaches owners before they're shopping. A restaurant owner won't Google "marketing agency" on a random Tuesday — but they will use a "how many reviews do I need to hit 4.5 stars?" calculator. That's how we catch them at the top of the funneliTop of the funnel = people who have a problem but aren't yet looking to buy. Reach them early and you're the name they remember when they are ready. and capture the email.

2. It earns backlinks cheaply. People link to useful free tools, never to sales pages. Each link lifts the ranking of every page we owniLinks are trust votes that raise our whole site's domain authority — the single lever a small site can pull to chase the giants. — the one lever that lets a small site close the gap to WebFX/Thrive.

3. It ranks for searches a service page can't. A tool ranks for "review calculator," "ROI calculator," etc. — words our /services page will never target.

Common misconception: "a free tool is pointless because it doesn't sell anything."   Reality: a tool isn't a salesperson — it's the front door. Its job isn't to close a deal; it's to capture a stranger's email and earn links. Two things make or break a tool: it has to be tied to a service we actually sell, and it has to capture the visitor's email. Get those right and the payoff is proven — the best-ranking competitor in this entire report is, essentially, a library of free tools.

What a relevant tool looks like for us: a free "Is your restaurant showing up on Google Maps?" checker, a "Google Business Profile health audit," or the review calculator we already have — each exposes a problem the owner didn't know they had, then hands them to us to fix it. That's the difference between a relevant tool and a random one.

2 · "How much does X cost?" pagesiA page that openly answers a pricing question, e.g. "How much does a restaurant website cost?" — explaining price ranges and what changes them.

You asked exactly what these are. Here's the full picture — they're the cheapest, fastest wins on the entire board.

📍 What they are + example

A single page that honestly answers a money question: "How much does a restaurant website cost?", "How much does local SEO cost per month?", "What do Google Ads cost for a small business?" — laying out realistic price ranges and what makes the number go up or down.

WebFX publishes a whole library of these ("how much does SEO cost," "digital marketing pricing") and ranks for all of them. The search "how much does a restaurant website cost" has confirmed, real monthly demand on Google right now.

✅ Why they convert so well

1. The searcher is almost ready to buy. Someone Googling "cost" has already decided they want the thing and is checking the price — that's the bottom of the funneliBottom of the funnel = people about to buy. They've stopped researching whether and are deciding who from. The highest-value visitor there is., the closest-to-the-wallet visitor there is.

2. Almost no competition. Most agencies refuse to talk price publicly. That silence is our opening — be the one honest answer on the page.

3. We don't even need a fixed price. A page giving honest ranges + "what changes the cost" + a "get your custom quote" button builds trust, ranks, and captures the lead — all at once.

Bottom line: one page each, low competition, and every visitor is a near-ready buyer. There is no faster, cheaper win in this whole report — and we currently have zero of them.

The rest of the gaps, in brief

3 · Hard result numbersiSpecific, real outcomes shown on the site — e.g. "+38% calls in 90 days" — instead of vague claims like "great results."

Example: Gourmet Marketing puts "$462M generated for partners · 17:1 ad ROI" right above the fold; WebFX shows exact "% traffic lift" per client.

Why it matters: owners don't trust adjectives, they trust numbers. "We got Grill Shack 38% more calls in 90 days" converts a browser into a lead far better than "amazing results." We currently show none — ask the team for real client wins.

4 · Publishing the blog

Example: Thrive publishes roughly daily — every article is another fishing line into Google. We have 11 finished drafts targeting easy-win searches.

Why it matters: every published post is a new way to be found for free. Ours are written and paid for but sitting unpublished — that's revenue in a drawer. Publishing them is the highest-ROI hour we can spend; the work is already done.

5 · "Best / comparison" articlesiArticles like "12 Best Restaurant SEO Companies (2026)" that capture people comparing vendors before they choose one.

Example: Thrive ranks for "12 Best SEO Companies for Restaurants" — and lists itself at #1. Flying V Group does the same.

Why it matters: these catch people in compare-and-choose mode — high buying intent. If we don't write them, our competitors write them and leave us off the list entirely.

6 · Trust badges & review counts

Example: Thrive flashes "Inc. 5000 · 7 years running," Google Premier Partner, and "4.7★ on Clutch (100+ reviews)" on every page.

Why it matters: a stranger deciding whether to trust us scans for proof in seconds. Badges and review counts are instant credibility shortcuts. We should collect Google/Clutch reviews and display the Google Partner badge.

Two gaps we're actually ahead on (so they're not in the list above): our customized local city pages already beat Thrive's thin templated ones, and our technical SEO is clean. The only "gap" that's genuinely slow is backlinks/domain authority — that's earned over time, not built in a week.
